Takeover, a working draft. Part 1, scene 10.

The circle closes

A living system is taken over one step at a time. When the newcomer can make all of its own parts, what it was built on falls away.

Start with a living thing whose parts are all made under the control of one hereditary system. Call it the first system. In Graham Cairns-Smith’s account of the origin of life, the first system was mineral: crystals of clay whose patterns were copied as they grew.

Somewhere inside it, one step in making those parts acquires a second way of being done, using a material the first system had made for some other purpose. The new way is clumsy. It survives because it works under conditions where the old way does not. Once it has a foothold it improves, and the old way of doing that step is dropped.

Nothing in this is aimed at replacing anything. Each change earns its keep at once, and both systems go on working side by side in the same line of descent. The difference comes at the last step. When the second system controls the making of everything it is made of, the first has nothing left to do. Cairns-Smith’s words for that moment are that the circle was complete.

What is left cannot be taken apart the way it was put together. It looks as though it must have been built all at once, and it was not.

After A. G. Cairns-Smith, Genetic Takeover and the Mineral Origins of Life (Cambridge University Press, 1982), pp. 124, 410–411 and 414.
